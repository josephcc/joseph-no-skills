# Braun & Clarke (2006), digested

A working summary, in our own words, of *Using thematic analysis in psychology* (Qualitative
Research in Psychology 3(2), 77–101). It exists so the skill still has its method when the
full text is not on the machine. **It is a fallback, not a substitute**: when
`source/braun-clarke-2006.txt` exists, read that instead (SKILL.md, Step 0). Page numbers
refer to the journal pagination.

## Vocabulary (p. 79)

- **Data corpus**: everything collected for the project. **Data set**: the part used for one
  analysis (e.g., only the post-interviews, or every passage anywhere that touches one topic).
- **Data item**: one interview, one session, one document. In our terms, one transcript.
- **Data extract**: one coded chunk pulled out of a data item. Many are produced; few are
  printed.

## What thematic analysis is (pp. 79–81)

A method for identifying, analysing and reporting patterns of meaning across a data set. It is
theoretically flexible: it can sit inside a realist stance (participants' talk reflects their
experience), a constructionist one (talk is where meaning gets made), or a contextualist
middle. The authors argue it is a method in its own right, not merely a step inside grounded
theory, and that much analysis published under other names is thematic analysis in practice.
Whatever stance you take, say what it is; an unstated stance is usually a realist one by default.

They object to the phrase "themes emerged": themes do not sit in the data waiting to be found.
The analyst notices, selects, and constructs them, and the write-up should own that.

## Decisions to make before coding (pp. 81–86)

1. **What counts as a theme.** Something that captures an important pattern in relation to the
   research question. Prevalence matters but is not decisive: there is no percentage cutoff, a
   theme can be brief in some items and absent in others, and the key themes are not always the
   most frequent. Pick one unit for counting prevalence (per data item, per speaker, or per
   instance) and use it consistently. Their own example counted at the data-item level.
2. **Rich description of the whole set, or a detailed account of one aspect.** The first suits
   under-researched areas; the second suits a focused analytic question.
3. **Inductive (bottom-up, data-driven) or theoretical (top-down, analyst-driven).** Inductive
   coding does not try to fit data to an existing frame, but no analyst codes in a vacuum.
4. **Semantic or latent.** Semantic stays with what was explicitly said and then interprets its
   significance; latent looks for the assumptions and ideas that shape what was said.
5. **Epistemology.** Realist analyses can talk about motivation and experience directly;
   constructionist ones theorise the conditions that make such accounts available.

They also separate three kinds of question: the research question, the questions asked of
participants, and the questions that guide coding. These need not coincide, and the worst
analyses simply reuse the interview questions as the themes.

## The six phases (pp. 86–93, Table 1)

Analysis is recursive, not linear; you move back and forth between the data set, the coded
extracts, and the analysis you are writing. Writing starts in phase 1.

1. **Familiarise.** Transcribe if needed, read and re-read the whole data set actively, note
   early ideas. Do not skip or sample this phase; it is the bedrock. Transcripts made by
   others should be checked against the recording.
2. **Generate initial codes.** Work systematically through the entire data set, giving each item
   equal attention. Code for as many potential patterns as time allows; code inclusively, keeping
   some surrounding context; an extract may carry zero, one, or many codes. Collate all extracts
   for each code. Keep accounts that depart from the dominant story.
3. **Search for themes.** Sort codes into candidate themes and sub-themes; gather every relevant
   extract under each. Visual aids (tables, mind maps, a thematic map) help. A temporary
   "miscellaneous" pile is fine. Abandon nothing yet.
4. **Review themes.** Level one: read all extracts under each theme and ask whether they form a
   coherent pattern; rework, split, or discard themes that do not. Level two: re-read the entire
   data set, check the thematic map against it, and code anything missed. Use Patton's criteria:
   internal homogeneity (data within a theme cohere) and external heterogeneity (themes are
   clearly distinct). Stop refining when changes stop adding anything.
5. **Define and name themes.** Identify the essence of each theme and of the set; write a detailed
   analysis of each, and place it in the overall story. Sub-themes structure large themes. Test:
   can you state each theme's scope and content in a couple of sentences? Names should be
   concise and immediately informative.
6. **Produce the report.** Tell the story of the data convincingly, with enough extracts to
   evidence each theme; choose vivid extracts that clearly exemplify the point; embed them in an
   analytic narrative that makes an argument about the research question rather than paraphrasing
   them.

Questions to press on in the late phases: what does this theme mean, what assumptions underpin
it, what are its implications, what conditions gave rise to it, why is this talked about in
this way rather than another, and what overall story do the themes tell.

## Pitfalls (pp. 94–95)

1. Not analysing at all: extracts strung together with little or no narrative, or narrative that
   only paraphrases them.
2. Using the interview questions as the themes.
3. Weak or unconvincing themes: too much overlap, not internally coherent, too few extracts per
   theme, or a few vivid instances reified into a pattern (anecdotalism).
4. Mismatch between data and claims: extracts that do not support, or even contradict, the
   analytic point; no consideration of alternative readings or of variation and contradiction.
   A pattern is rarely 100% complete, and an analysis that implies so invites suspicion.
5. Mismatch between theoretical stance and claims, or a method section that does not say what was
   done and why.

## The 15-point quality checklist (p. 96, Table 2), paraphrased

Transcription
1. Transcripts are detailed enough and were checked against the recordings.

Coding
2. Every data item received equal attention.
3. Themes rest on thorough, inclusive coding across the set, not on a few vivid examples.
4. All relevant extracts for every theme were collated.
5. Themes were checked against each other and against the original data.
6. Themes are internally coherent, consistent, and distinct.

Analysis
7. Data were interpreted, not just paraphrased or described.
8. Analysis and extracts match: the extracts illustrate the claims.
9. The analysis tells a convincing, well-organised story.
10. Analytic narrative and illustrative extracts are in balance.

Overall
11. Enough time was given to every phase.

Written report
12. The assumptions and the specific form of thematic analysis are stated.
13. What you say you did matches what you show you did.
14. The report's language fits the stated epistemology.
15. The researcher is positioned as active; themes do not "emerge".

## Advantages and limits (pp. 97)

Flexible, quick to learn, accessible, good at summarising a large body of data and at showing
similarities and differences, can produce unanticipated insight. Limits: without an anchoring
framework it stays descriptive; it loses the continuity of any one person's account (unlike
narrative methods); it cannot make claims about language use itself (unlike discourse or
conversation analysis). Choose the method for the question, not the other way round.
